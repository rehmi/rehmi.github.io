Overview

Two small systems hacks from the early 1980s, posted to Usenet from the University of Maryland, College Park. One exposed a hole in how Unix guarded kernel memory and helped get it closed; the other borrowed a convenience from TOPS-20 and stitched it into the BSD terminal driver. Both survive: the original postings are archived below, and so are the complete kernel patches for the second, as they went out on net.sources in 1985.

A keystroke snooper that hardened /dev/kmem (1982)

In December 1982 a short program went out to net.unix-wizards under the subject line "I felt slightly obnoxious, so…." It read another user's terminal input — the raw clist/cblock buffers the kernel used to hold typed characters — straight out of kernel memory through /dev/kmem, which on the Unix systems of the day was world-readable. By reassembling those buffers it could reconstruct whatever someone else was typing at their terminal, passwords included: the login program's getpass() did nothing to hide the keystrokes from a reader with access to kernel memory.

The point was never the snooping; it was the hole it made obvious. Within days, Jay Lepreau at the University of Utah replied in the same thread (12 December 1982):

It was only a matter of time till one of these became widely available. Ok, so we finally must do what we should have long ago: protect mem/kmem & make all the mem pgms setgid or uid.

He went on to name the memory-reading utilities — ps, w, vmstat, pstat and their kin — that would have to become setgid so that /dev/mem and /dev/kmem could finally be read-protected in future distributions. That is roughly what came to pass: world-readable kernel memory was locked down, and the tools that genuinely needed it were made privileged. A hole closed because someone made it too easy to ignore.

A twenex-style ^T for the BSD tty driver (1983–1985)

The idea comes from TOPS-20 (TWENEX): press ^T (Ctrl-T) at the terminal, when not in raw mode, and the system prints a one-line report on itself and on the job in the foreground. The BSD version began as a hook in the 4.1BSD terminal driver. The revision log at the head of the patched tty.c opens with the entry

print out primitive system stats (for now) rehmi 18 Apr 83

and over the next sixteen months records clearer statistics, a shorter message, priorities on process states, and one-second timeouts, with a few fixes signed by colleagues ("chris", "james"). In August 1984 the code moved twice in a week: to Pyramid's dual-universe OSx on 5 August, and from there to BRL's 4.2BSD on the Vax on 10 August.

In March 1985 a short note to net.unix-wizards (itself a repost) offered it by mail, with an example of the output:

up 2h 16m, load 6.20 4.19, %cpu 0.07, 6k of 47k, kbd wait

The patch is small and touches only the terminal code: a USESTAT compile option, a new t_usest character in the tty structure (so the trigger key can be reassigned like any other), and a ttystatout() routine in tty.c that composes the report. Eleven meters can be switched on or off per terminal through stty — the 1-, 5- and 15-minute load averages, uptime, the process-group leader and its children, their CPU share (raw or cooked), their size and resident set, their flags, and a rough state. The state is the most telling one across the whole foreground process group, ranked in a small priority table: "kbd wait" (asleep on the terminal's own input queue) outranks "running", which outranks "idling", "waiting", "swapped", "stopped", and "zombied". "Losing" is held in reserve for when no process turns up at all.

In June 1985 Chris Torek, also of the Maryland computer science department, posted the full kernel and stty changes to net.sources in three versions — 4.1BSD, 4.2BSD on the Vax, and the Pyramid kernel — and announced them in net.unix-wizards as "our (Rehmi's really) kernel & stty mods." His preface to the patches puts it plainly:

Most of the code was written by Rehmi Post (who also made up the diff listings).

Those postings are why the code survives at all. The same idea — a status line summoned from the terminal driver — later became the familiar BSD ^T / SIGINFO feature, where a keystroke prints the load average and the foreground process's state. Whether this patch is an ancestor of that feature or a parallel take on the same TWENEX idea isn't documented, so no lineage is claimed here.

Context

Both date from an early stretch of Unix systems hacking at Maryland — the same College Park years as the ZMOB parallel-processing work — posted under the handle "Khron The Elder" and later under his own name to a net.unix-wizards readership that took in much of the era's BSD community. Minor artifacts, but honest ones: a security curiosity that helped close a real hole, and a terminal convenience the system later caught up to.
